📄 Computational Thinking & Literacy
+ 1. Introduction: Rethinking Computational Thinking
§	Status: Computing shapes modern society, requiring new ways of thinking and problem-solving.
§	Problem: Education separates computer science from literacy, treating coding as a technical skill.
§	Central Argument: Computational Thinking (CT) should be understood as a form of literacy.
§	Framework: The paper proposes a three-dimensional model:
1)	CT as literacy
2)	CT through literacy
3)	Literacy through CT
2. What is Computational Thinking?
§	Definition: A general problem-solving approach applicable across disciplines.
§	Key Skills
1)	Navigating multiple levels of abstraction
2)	Decomposing problems into manageable pieces
3)	Algorithmic thinking
4)	Representing data through models
§	Core Insight: CT focuses on how to think, not just how to code
3. Literacy: A Sociocultural Perspective
§	Traditional View: Literacy = reading and writing
§	Expanded View (Sociocultural): Literacy = socially situated practices using tools and technologies for meaning-making and expression (Barton & Hamilton, 1998; Gee, 1996)
§	Key Insight: Literacy evolves with technology (e.g., printing press → digital media) (Warschauer, 1999), and because digital environments require abstraction, sequencing, and rule-based reasoning, computational thinking becomes an integral part of literacy
4. Computational Thinking as Literacy
§	Core Idea: CT is a new literacy embedded in modern sociotechnical practices
§	Material Dimension:
1)	Computers act as a meta-medium enabling expression
2)	Technology reshapes communication practices
§	Cognitive Dimension: CT enhances thinking through core processes:
1)	Abstraction: focusing on essential features while simplifying complexity 
2)	Automation: creating rule-based procedures that can be executed by machines 
3)	Analysis: examining patterns and processes to evaluate and refine solutions
§	Social Dimension: CT is shaped by cultural, institutional, and technological contexts
§	Key Insight: Like print literacy, CT transforms how people think and communicate
5. Computational Thinking through Literacy
§	Core Idea: Existing literacy skills can support the development of CT
§	Mechanisms:
1)	Pseudocode: bridges natural language and programming logic by expressing human ideas in a structured, algorithmic form that is easy to understand yet logically precise.
2)	Narratives mirror computational processes in that both organize events into structured sequences with clear causality (e.g., beginning–middle–end parallels step-by-step algorithmic logic).
3)	Writing processes (drafting, revising) parallel coding (design, debugging)
§	Pedagogical Tools: Scratch, Alice (story-based programming environments)
§	Key Insight: Learners use language knowledge to understand computational structures
6. Literacy through Computational Thinking
§	Core Idea: CT can enhance both traditional literacy (reading/writing) and new literacy (digital, multimodal) by providing structured ways to organize meaning and expression.
§	Applications:
1)	Form–Meaning–Use parallel between programming and language → Programming, like language, involves form (syntax), meaning (semantics), and use (function in context).
2)	Sequencing supports grammar and writing organization → Writing coherent sentences and texts requires logical ordering, similar to step-by-step instructions in programming.
3)	Debugging promotes reflection and metacognition → Identifying and correcting errors in code mirrors revising writing.
§	Key Insight: Programming strengthens language learning by reinforcing structure and meaning
7. New Media and Production
§	Shift: From consuming media → producing digital content
§	Problem: Students often use pre-built tools without understanding computation
§	Solution: CT enables learners to become creators of digital environments

8. Identity, Motivation, and Equity
§	Motivation: Programming supports creativity and self-expression
§	Identity: Learners become active creators, not passive users
§	Equity Issue: Access to CT is uneven across social groups
§	Implication: Integrating CT into literacy broadens participation
9. Educational Implications
§	Integration Strategy: Combine CT with literacy instruction by embedding computational practices (e.g., sequencing, pattern recognition, algorithmic thinking) into reading, writing, and communication activities, rather than teaching them as separate subjects.
§	Benefit: 
1)	Enhances both computational and language skills
2)	Aligns education with real-world demands
10. Conclusion
§	Main Claim: CT is a fundamental literacy of the 21st century
§	Contribution: Integrates computer science, literacy, and new media studies
§	Future Direction: Develop curriculum, assessment, and interdisciplinary approaches
🔥 Overall Summary
§	Computational thinking operates in three interconnected ways:
1)	as literacy (a new form of meaning-making)
2)	through literacy (built on language skills)
3)	for literacy (enhancing language and communication)
👉 CT is ultimately a new paradigm for thinking, learning, and expressing in a digital society.
