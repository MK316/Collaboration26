# AI Competency Framework for Teachers by UNESCO  

##  1.1 Why an AI Competency Framework? 
 - Potential of AI: to revolutionize education by processing vast amounts of information, generating new content, and facilitating decision-making  
 - Risk
 1)  Threatening human agency
 2)  Violating data privacy
 3)  Deepening systemic inequalities
 4)  Devaluing the essential teacher-student relationship
 - teachers must be empowered to understand the technical, ethical, and pedagogical dimensions of AI
 - As of 2022, only seven countries had developed national AI frameworks for teachers, highlighting a critical global gap in guidance and training.
 - Aim: to fill this gap by advocating a human-centered approach where AI supports, rather than replaces, human interaction and flourishing

 ## 1.2 Purpose and Target Audience 
 - The framework is designed for general subject teachers who use AI to facilitate learning, rather than for specialized AI experts
- Its primary objectives are to serve as a global reference for developing national or institutional competency frameworks, to provide a planning tool for teacher educators, and to establish a benchmarking matrix for assessing teachers' professional learning and skills

## 1.3 Alignment with the ICT Competency Framework for Teachers 
- The AI CFT is aligned with and complements the 2018 UNESCO ICT Competency Framework for Teachers (ICT CFT)
. Both frameworks share a common architecture based on five aspects of competency across three progression levels
. This alignment ensures a holistic approach to professional development, covering pre-service training, in-service preparation, and ongoing support in a consistent manner

## 1.4 Technological Advances in AI and Implications for Teacher Competencies 
- AI presents unique challenges that distinguish it from previous generations of ICT tools:
1) Human Agency: Unlike deterministic tools, AI can mimic human behavior and replace decision-making, which risks the atrophy of essential teaching competencies if human agency is not prioritized
2) Privacy and Ethics: AI relies on aggressive data mining that often exploits personal data, requiring teachers to have a deeper understanding of ethical issues to protect students
3) Stochastic Nature: AI outputs are probabilistic (stochastic) rather than certain, meaning they can be untrustworthy or produce "hallucinations"
- Teachers must develop the capacity to critically examine AI-generated content
- Transformative Potential: AI functions as a general-purpose technology that can transform social and professional practices
- Teachers need to understand these social impacts and motivate themselves through continuous, lifelong professional learning

## 2.1 Ensuring inclusive digital futures 
- Ensuring equitable digital futures must be grounded in a human and social foundation where the design and use of AI are strictly human-led
- Teachers need the critical capacity to debunk AI hype, understanding that only intentionally ethical design can enhance human capabilities and sustainability
- It is vital to ensure that human and social values, such as empathy and justice, prevail over profit-driven algorithms to maintain social cohesion

## 2.2 A human-centred approach to AI AI should be at the service of strengthening human capacities and must be equitable, safe, and explainable
- A core tenet is human accountability, meaning AI tools should never replace the legitimate responsibility of teachers in pedagogical decisions
- Furthermore, teachers must ensure AI is used in an inclusive manner, protecting all students regardless of gender, ethnicity, or ability

## 2.3 Protecting teachers’ rights and iteratively (re)defining teachers’ roles 
- The interaction between teachers and learners remains the core of education, and AI must not be designed to replace this relationship
- Policy-makers should urgently redefine teachers' roles as collaborative knowledge producers and guides to citizenship in the AI era
- Legal protections and long-term financial commitments are necessary to ensure teachers have inclusive access to these vital technological resources

## 2.4 Promoting trustworthy and environmentally sustainable AI for education 
- AI systems must be validated for safety and trustworthiness at a national level before being adopted in classrooms
- This includes mandating the 'do no harm' principle, which considers potential impacts on human rights and environmental sustainability, such as carbon emissions
- Educational AI must be reliable, age-appropriate, and secure, particularly for students with special needs

## 2.5 Ensuring applicability for all teachers and reflecting digital evolution 
- The framework is designed to be universally applicable, supporting all teachers regardless of their starting point or digital expertise
- It provides guidance for a confident transition from traditional ICT tools to more advanced AI systems
- By fostering transferable competencies, the framework prepares teachers to respond effectively to the rapid and continuous upgrading of AI technologies

## 2.6 Lifelong professional learning for teachers 
- Teacher development is viewed as a continuous, lifelong journey
- This involves constant reflection and improvement of practical performance through iterative cycles of lesson design and implementation
- To support this, institutions should streamline training programmes (pre-service and in-service) and adapt policies to provide sufficient time and incentives for teachers to engage in ongoing professional growth
